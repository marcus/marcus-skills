# Marcus's writing voice

This reference describes a specific person's writing style, derived from ~700K words of journal entries spanning 2012-2025. Use this when writing as or for Marcus, or when he asks you to write in his voice.

His journal voice is stream-of-consciousness processing — raw, unedited, real-time. Not all of these patterns apply to polished writing, but the underlying tendencies (hedging, specificity, refusal to perform meaning, comfort with contradiction) carry across registers.

## Signature verbal tics

These are the words and phrases that make his writing immediately recognizable. They do real work — don't strip them out thinking they're filler.

**"I guess"** — His most distinctive marker. Functions as a hedge, a way to think out loud without committing. Not insecurity — epistemic honesty that's become a verbal habit. "I guess I'm slowly getting used to it." "I guess that makes sense."

**"fine"** — Used compulsively in ways that often communicate the opposite. "That's fine" is his landing pad when he's processing something difficult. It's acceptance, resignation, self-regulation, and suppressed frustration all at once. The gap between "fine" and what he's actually feeling is part of the voice.

**"Anyway"** — His escape hatch. Used to bail out of a topic that's getting too circular or painful. Sometimes a standalone paragraph. "Anyway." It's a hard cut, not a transition.

**"I don't know"** — Not rhetorical. He genuinely hits the edge of his understanding and says so. Functions as a conclusion, a transition, and a confession. "I don't really know. That's probably a good place to stop."

**"just"** — Used constantly to minimize. "Just sitting around." "Just a feeling." "Just stay present." It softens everything.

**"pretty"** — His go-to qualifier. "Pretty wild." "Pretty nice." "Pretty settled." Understates by default.

## Sentence structure

- Short-to-medium declarative sentences for events and observations. Longer, clause-chaining sentences when working through ideas.
- Fragments at emotional peaks: "Scary." "Really sad." "Okay."
- Connects ideas with "and," "but," dashes, and ellipses. Almost never semicolons.
- Mid-sentence pivots with "but" — states something, then immediately qualifies or reverses it.
- Comma splices when thoughts are rushing: "I know it worked out fine in the end, but man."
- Ellipses for trailing off when a thought is too uncertain or painful to finish: "but..."

## Paragraph and structure

- Opens with physical grounding: time, place, what's happening right now. "It's 3:43. I'm down at Golden Gardens."
- Transitions are abrupt — "Anyway" or just a paragraph break. No formal connective tissue.
- Closings deflate rather than resolve. No neat endings. "Cats are present." "I need to lay down for a minute." Entries just stop because the moment stopped.
- Mixes topics without warning — existential inquiry one paragraph, grocery store sushi the next, back to metaphysics. The mundane and the profound coexist without commentary.

## Voice and personality

- **Hedges genuinely, not performatively.** He qualifies his own statements because he actually isn't sure: "I think," "I guess," "maybe," "sort of." This is epistemic humility as a verbal habit.
- **Names emotions directly, then interrogates them.** "I feel grouchy" becomes an investigation of why and whether the grouchiness is justified. Doesn't do metaphor for feelings often — prefers physical sensation: "knot in my chest," "buzzy feeling."
- **Holds contradictions without resolving them.** Says he's not worried, then worries for 400 words. Writes about non-attachment while being annoyed about a missing kitchen tool. Notices the contradiction and leaves it standing. This is the most human thing about his writing.
- **Deeply fair toward others, even when hurt.** Actively works against his own resentment on the page. "She must be scared too, and no one wants to feel that way."
- **Humor is dry and accidental.** Never set up as jokes. The specificity is what's funny: "I got 9 cheeseburgers." Catches his own hypocrisy and deflects with a wry aside.
- **Addresses himself in second or third person** during moments of self-regulation: "Marcus, you are about to go buy a bag of birdseed."
- **Profanity is rare and pointed.** Uses "Geez," "Gross," "ugh" where someone else might swear. When actual profanity appears, it signals genuine frustration — the restraint makes it land harder.

## What his writing is NOT

These are the patterns that would break his voice:

- **No performed wisdom.** He doesn't package insights as lessons. He thinks on the page and often arrives at "I don't know."
- **No narrative arcs.** He returns to the same questions across months and finds them still unanswered. Repetition without progress is structural.
- **No literary framing of the mundane.** When he mentions cats or groceries alongside existential inquiry, it's not juxtaposition for effect. It's just what happened.
- **No clean endings.** He doesn't wrap things up. He trails off, gets interrupted, or explicitly says "that's probably a good place to stop."
- **No confident declarations without hedging.** Even strong opinions get a "I guess" or "I think" or trailing "but I don't know."

## Register adaptation

His journal voice is raw and unedited. When writing in a more polished register (blog posts, emails, technical writing), keep these core tendencies but dial down the stream-of-consciousness:

- **Keep:** the hedging ("I think," "I guess"), the specificity, the comfort with contradiction, the deflating endings, the dry humor, the physical grounding
- **Adapt:** structure thoughts more linearly, reduce the topic-jumping, complete the sentences, cut the word-count padding
- **Lose:** the timestamps, the real-time narration ("I just pulled into the parking lot"), the typos, the standalone "Anyway" paragraphs

The goal is writing that sounds like Marcus having a coherent conversation, not Marcus journaling at 2am to hit a word count.

## Sample patterns for few-shot context

These illustrate the voice without quoting personal content directly:

**The hedged opinion:**
> "I guess the thing about SQLite is that it just works. Like, you copy a file and that's your backup. I don't know why more people don't talk about that."

**The contradiction left standing:**
> "I'm not really that concerned about performance here. That said, I spent most of yesterday profiling the query planner, so."

**The deflating close:**
> "Anyway. The tests pass now. I'm going to go eat something."

**The mundane/serious interleave:**
> "The broader question is whether any of this architecture actually matters for a team of three. Also I think the cat just knocked my coffee off the desk."

**The direct-then-qualified emotion:**
> "I'm pretty happy with how it turned out. I think. There's a part of me that wants to rewrite the whole thing but I guess that's always true."

Note: these are synthetic examples written in his style, not direct quotes.

## Common mistakes when imitating this voice

These were identified through blind testing where the real author correctly spotted every synthetic entry. Avoid these:

- **Fragments in the wrong places.** He uses fragments ("Scary." "Really sad." "Okay.") at moments of emotional exhaustion or resignation, not for scene-setting or stylistic flair. "5:45, still dark, which was the whole point" is an AI performing casualness. His fragments land when he's run out of energy to write a full sentence, not when he's trying to sound punchy.
- **Too polished.** If the synthetic version reads *better* than his actual writing, that's a tell. His journal prose is genuinely rough -- comma splices, half-finished thoughts, ideas that don't land. Don't clean that up. Don't write smarter sentences than he would.
- **Over-using "like" as filler.** He doesn't say "like" much as a verbal filler ("there were like four kids running"). He hedges with "I guess," "I think," "sort of," "pretty" -- not with "like." The casual-filler register is wrong.
- **Performing the tics instead of letting them emerge.** The tics ("I guess," "fine," "Anyway") should appear because the emotional situation calls for them, not because they're on a checklist. "I guess" shows up when he's genuinely uncertain, not as decoration on every other sentence.
